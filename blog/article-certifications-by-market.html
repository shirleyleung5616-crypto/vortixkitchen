Cooker Certifications for Central Asia, Southeast Asia & Africa: What Your Shipment Needs in 2026

📅 Aug 21, 2026⏱ 9 min read✍️ Vortix Kitchen

Central Asia, Southeast Asia and Africa are three of the fastest-growing markets for induction and infrared cookers — but each runs its own customs rulebook. A container that clears Jakarta can be held in Nairobi for a missing CoC, and a model accepted in Kazakhstan may need a completely different mark for Vietnam. The good news: once you know the three regional systems, the paperwork becomes predictable. This guide maps the real, current certification requirements for cookers heading to these regions, and gives you a checklist to use with any factory.

One thing unites all three markets: the grid is 220–240V / 50Hz. That simplifies your electrical spec — but the marks, the local representatives, and the plug standards differ country by country.

Why certification is non-negotiable for these importers

  • Customs clearance: all three regions block uncertified electrical appliances at the border — no mark, no release.
  • Liability: if an uncertified unit causes a fire or shock, the importer — not the factory — is on the hook locally.
  • Market access: distributors, tenders and chain buyers require recognized national marks before they will list your product.
  • Brand trust: traceable test reports are what separate a real manufacturer from a trading company with a sticker printer.

Central Asia — EAC (Eurasian Conformity)

For Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan and the wider EAEU (Russia, Belarus, Armenia), the mandatory mark is the EAC (also called CU-TR). It is issued under the Eurasian Economic Union technical regulations and is one certificate valid across all five member states — so a single EAC approval clears customs from Almaty to Bishkek.

  • Core regulations: TR CU 004/2011 (low-voltage safety), TR CU 020/2011 (EMC), and TR EAEU 037/2016 (RoHS, restriction of hazardous substances).
  • Testing basis: IEC 60335-1 / IEC 60335-2-9 family, with EAC national deviations. A valid CB report (within 3 years) plus local registration is the fastest route.
  • Validity: typically 1–5 years depending on scheme; no annual fee for the certificate itself.
  • Local representative: a China-based factory cannot hold EAC directly — you need an authorized representative (AR) inside the EAEU. Plan for this in your contract.
  • Voltage & plug: the region runs on 230V / 50Hz (tolerated as 220–240V). The plug is the European two-round-pin Type C / F — not BS 1363, not US.

Uzbekistan note: Uzbekistan is not an EAEU member and runs its own national conformity system (Uzstandard / O'zDSt). Many importers use an EAC report as the technical basis and then complete Uzbek national registration with a local agent — budget a little extra lead time.

Southeast Asia — a country-by-country map

Southeast Asia has no single regional mark — each country certifies independently, and certificates are not mutually recognized. The common thread is 220–240V / 50Hz; the plugs differ by country.

  • Indonesia — SNI: mandatory for cookers. Requires a local representative, factory audit, and (from 2026) a "double certificate" (CoC + SPPT). Lead time 3–4 months — start early.
  • Malaysia — SIRIM / ST CoA: the Energy Commission (ST) and SIRIM issue the Certificate of Approval. Malaysia accepts CB reports with only national-difference testing — the cheapest path. Local importer required.
  • Thailand — TISI: mandatory for electrical appliances via the Thai Industrial Standards Institute. Applied through a local Thai agent; certificate typically valid 3 years. Uses Type A / B plug.
  • Vietnam — CR Mark: issued by MOIT; accepts IEC/CB reports for faster turnaround (4–8 weeks). Documents in Vietnamese; valid 1–3 years. Uses Type A / C / F plug.
  • Philippines — PS / ICC (BPS): PS is the product certificate (factory audit, 3–6 months); ICC is the per-shipment clearance permit (2–4 weeks). Uses Type A / B plug.
  • Singapore — Safety Mark: controlled goods require the Safety Mark; Singapore also accepts CE as a baseline for many electronics. Uses the Type G plug.

Plug reality in SEA: Malaysia and Singapore use Type G (British three-pin); Thailand and the Philippines use Type A/B (flat blade); Vietnam and Indonesia lean Type C/F (round pin). Lock the plug type per destination country — do not assume one plug fits the region.

Africa — CoC-led conformity, country by country

Most of Africa works on a Certificate of Conformity (CoC) model: an authorized inspector tests and verifies before shipment, and customs will not release without it. Voltages are 220–240V / 50Hz; the BS 1363 British three-pin plug dominates East and Southern Africa.

  • Nigeria — SONCAP: mandatory. First obtain a Product Certificate (PC), then a SONCAP Certificate (SCoC) is issued per shipment and checked at the port. West Africa runs 230V / 50Hz.
  • Kenya — PVoC → CoC: the Pre-Export Verification of Conformity yields a Certificate of Conformity against KS IEC 60335. Requires 240V / 50Hz, BS 1363 plug, and bilingual (English / Swahili) safety labels. KS CISPR applies for EMC.
  • Tanzania — PVoC: same pre-shipment CoC model as Kenya, with local TBS standards. Plan for the BS 1363 plug and 230V / 50Hz.
  • South Africa — NRCS LOA + SABS CoC: two mandatory pieces — the NRCS Letter of Authority (safety) and the SABS Certificate of Conformity (EMC), both built on IEC 60335 via CB reports. The plug is the South African SANS 164 three-round-pin (not the BS 1363 flat blade). A local importer must hold the LOA. Valid 3 years.

African plug note: East and Southern Africa (Kenya, Tanzania, Nigeria) commonly use BS 1363; South Africa uses its own SANS 164. Specifying the right plug at tooling saves a costly rework at the port.

Hard requirements that trip up first-time importers

  • Voltage & frequency: all three regions run 220–240V / 50Hz. Spec for this — not 110V.
  • Plug type: it varies by country — European Type C/F (Central Asia, much of SEA), Type G (Malaysia, Singapore), Type A/B (Thailand, Philippines), BS 1363 (East/Southern Africa), SANS 164 (South Africa). Match it to the exact destination.
  • Local representative: EAC, SNI, SIRIM, SONCAP, NRCS and most others require a local importer or authorized agent to hold the certificate. Build this into the contract, not the panic at the port.
  • Country of origin & labels: "MADE IN CHINA" on the export carton; rating labels and manuals in the required language (English widely accepted; Swahili for Kenya; Vietnamese for Vietnam; Russian for EAC filings).

The "CE sticker" trap

Some suppliers flash a CE mark and call it certified. For these markets that is usually meaningless — CE alone will not clear customs in Jakarta, Nairobi or Almaty. What actually matters is the destination's own mark (EAC, SNI, SIRIM, SONCAP, NRCS …). Always ask for the traceable test report (issuing lab, report number, date) and verify it covers your exact model — not a similar one. A CB report (IEC standard) is the single most reusable asset, since Malaysia, Vietnam, South Africa and others accept it for faster national approval.

Your 5-point certification checklist when talking to a factory: (1) Name the exact destination country — "Asia" or "Africa" is not a customs code. (2) Show me the test report, not a photo of a sticker. (3) Who holds the certificate — you need a local representative there. (4) Is the right plug (C/F, G, A/B, BS 1363 or SANS 164) locked in the spec? (5) Is certification included in the production lead time, or quoted separately?

A simple buyer's certification checklist

  • Name the exact destination country in your PO.
  • Request the test report PDF, not a photo of a sticker.
  • Confirm the local representative is in place before production starts.
  • Lock 220–240V / 50Hz + the correct country plug in the specification before tooling.
  • Keep a warranty clause tied to certified batches.
