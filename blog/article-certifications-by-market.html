Your Cooker Container Is Sitting at the Port. Here's How to Make Sure It Never Happens.

📅 Aug 21, 2026⏱ 9 min read✍️ Vortix Kitchen

An induction or infrared cooker is cheap. A container of them stuck at Mombasa, Lagos or Jakarta for a missing certificate is not. Demurrage runs from the first day the ship is held; the bigger loss is the season you miss and the buyers who walk. For importers into Central Asia, Southeast Asia and Africa, customs holds are the single most expensive mistake in the whole deal — and almost all of them are preventable at the quotation stage, not at the port.

This is not a certification encyclopedia. It is the six checks we run with every importer before a single unit is tooled — the ones that keep containers moving.

What actually gets a cooker container held

Three things, in order of how often we see them:

  • No valid national mark. CE is not a passport here. A unit with only a CE sticker is treated as uncertified in Nairobi, Almaty and most of SEA — the local mark is what customs wants.
  • Right product, wrong plug. A 220–240V unit is electrically fine, but if the plug doesn't match the destination country, some ports treat the whole shipment as non-compliant.
  • No local representative on file. Most of these markets require a named local importer or agent to hold the certificate. If that name isn't on the paperwork, clearance stalls even when the product is perfect.

The 6 checks to run before you sign the PO

Treat this as your pre-shipment gate. If any answer is "I don't know," fix it before production — not after the container sails.

  1. Name the exact destination country. "Asia" and "Africa" are not customs codes. EAC, SNI, SIRIM, TISI, CR, ICC, SONCAP, PVoC and NRCS are all different schemes for different countries. The factory can only spec correctly if it knows the precise landing port.
  2. Ask for the test report — not a photo of a sticker. A real report has an issuing lab, a report number and a date, and covers your exact model. A CB report (IEC 60335 basis) is the most reusable asset because Malaysia, Vietnam and South Africa accept it for faster national approval.
  3. Lock the plug at the tooling stage. Voltage is uniform (220–240V / 50Hz across all three regions) but plugs are not: Type C/F in Central Asia and much of SEA, Type G in Malaysia and Singapore, Type A/B in Thailand and the Philippines, BS 1363 in East/Southern Africa, SANS 164 in South Africa. Changing it after production means a rework — or a hold.
  4. Confirm the local representative is in place. EAC, SNI, SIRIM, SONCAP and NRCS all require a local entity to hold the certificate. Put that name in the contract, not in a panic email at the port.
  5. Build certification lead time into your calendar. Indonesia's SNI alone can run 3–4 months with a factory audit; Philippines ICC is faster but still weeks. If your supplier quotes cert as "included" with no timeline, assume the worst and plan the shipment around it.
  6. Get country-of-origin and labels right on the carton. "MADE IN CHINA" on the export carton; rating labels and manuals in the required language (English widely accepted; Swahili for Kenya; Vietnamese for Vietnam; Russian for EAC filings).

The one trap that fools first-time importers

Some suppliers flash a CE mark and call the unit "certified." For these markets that is usually meaningless. CE alone will not clear customs in Jakarta, Nairobi or Almaty. What matters is the destination's own mark. The fastest way to lose a shipment is to trust the sticker and skip the verification in check #2.

What each region needs — in one line

  • Central Asia (Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, EAEU): one EAC certificate clears all five member states — but Uzbekistan runs its own system, so budget extra lead time there.
  • Southeast Asia: no regional mark. SNI (Indonesia, slowest), SIRIM (Malaysia, cheapest via CB), TISI (Thailand), CR (Vietnam), ICC/PS (Philippines), Safety Mark (Singapore).
  • Africa: CoC-led — SONCAP (Nigeria, PC + per-shipment SCoC), PVoC → CoC (Kenya, Tanzania), NRCS LOA + SABS CoC (South Africa). BS 1363 plug dominates East/Southern Africa; South Africa uses SANS 164.
The container that sails without a local rep named, the right plug locked, and a real test report on file is the one that sits at the port. Run the six checks above and you remove 90% of the risk before production even starts.
