Induction vs Infrared Cooker: Which One Fits Your Market?

📅 Sep 5, 2026⏱ 8 min read✍️ Vortix Kitchen

Induction and infrared cookers look almost identical on a shelf: flat glass top, touch panel, same carton size. But inside they work in completely different ways — and that difference decides whether your container sells or sits. This guide gives you the full comparison: how each technology heats, where each one wins, and how importers across Africa, Southeast Asia, Central Asia and Russia decide the right mix.

The real difference: what actually heats the food

Induction creates a rapidly changing magnetic field under the glass. When a magnetic-bottom pot (cast iron, enameled steel, most stainless steel) sits on the zone, the field induces currents inside the pot itself — the pot becomes the heater. The glass stays comparatively cool, and no energy is wasted heating the cooking surface first.

Infrared (ceramic/radiant) works like a giant glowing hotplate: electric resistance coils heat the glass-ceramic plate until it glows red, and the hot glass transfers heat to whatever you place on top. The technology does not care what the pot is made of.

One heats the pot directly; the other heats a plate that heats the pot. Everything else in this comparison follows from that single mechanical difference.

When infrared is the right call

In much of Africa and Southeast Asia, the default pot is aluminum — and aluminum is not magnetic. Put it on an induction cooker and either nothing happens or the unit flashes an error code. To a first-time buyer that reads as "it doesn't work", and "it doesn't work" becomes your return. Infrared works with the pots people already own, which removes that entire return category.

Infrared also wins when:

  • Your buyers want the lowest shelf price — infrared builds are simpler and cheaper.
  • Your market uses round-bottom pots or clay pots that never sit flat on an induction zone.
  • You need a low-risk first order: universal compatibility means fewer confused customers.

The trade-offs: slower to reach temperature, more electricity for the same cooking result, and a surface that stays dangerously hot after cooking — worth a warning sticker and a line in your manual.

When induction is the right call

Central Asia and Russia largely cook with magnetic-bottom pots, so induction's main barrier does not apply there. In those markets induction sells as the modern, premium option:

  • Speed: water boils noticeably faster — a shop demonstration that sells itself.
  • Efficiency: 85–90% of the electricity reaches the food, a real saving where power is billed by the kWh.
  • Safety story: no open glow, cool-touch surface, auto-off when the pot is removed — the features parents and restaurant owners both ask about.
  • Margin: higher perceived technology supports a higher price tier than infrared.

The engineering requirement is inside the unit: induction runs on high-frequency electronics, and the IGBT and control board quality decide whether units survive unstable grids, dust and long duty cycles. That is why factory selection and burn-in testing matter more for induction than for infrared.

Commercial kitchens: a separate decision

Restaurants, canteens and street-food operators choose differently from households. Commercial buyers care about power (3.5 kW and up), duty cycle and repairability. High-power commercial induction dominates professional wok cooking because of speed and instant control; infrared flat-tops serve buffets and holding stations where any pot or pan must work. If you sell to foodservice, treat commercial induction and commercial infrared as two separate lines with separate specs — do not reuse consumer logic.

So which is better? The honest answer

Neither. The right question is: which pots does your market already own?

  • Aluminum, clay or round-bottom pots dominant → lead with infrared.
  • Magnetic-bottom pots common (much of Central Asia, Russia, modern urban retail) → lead with induction as your premium line.
  • Mixed or unknown → carry both: infrared as the volume entry line, induction as the margin line, and let a small test order tell you.

Importers who carry both rarely regret it. The two technologies rarely compete for the same customer — they cover different buyers, and together they cover the whole market.

What to write in your PO

  • Induction lines: "magnetic-bottom cookware only; no-pot / wrong-pot auto shutoff; branded IGBT; 100% power-on burn-in before packing; wide-voltage option (100–240V) for mixed markets."
  • Infrared lines: "any-cookware radiant plate; overheat protection; hot-surface warning label on glass and carton."
  • Both: confirm plug type and voltage/frequency per destination country before production — the cheapest fix to agree early, the most expensive to discover late.

Buyer questions we hear every week

Can I use aluminum pots on an induction cooker? No — aluminum is not magnetic, so the unit detects no pot and shuts off (usually an E0-style code). The pot needs a magnetic flat bottom, or you use an infrared cooker instead.

Why does my induction cooker reject some steel pots? Some stainless mixes are not magnetic enough for the sensor. If a magnet sticks firmly to the pot bottom, the pot will work.

Do infrared cookers use more electricity? For the same cooking result, yes. Induction transfers roughly 85–90% of its power into the food; infrared around 60–70%, because the glass plate itself must be heated first.

Is the induction glass really safe to touch? It stays far cooler than an infrared plate because the glass is not the heater — but it warms from the pot sitting on it, so "cool" never means "cold".

Which should a first-time importer start with? Match your market's pots (see above). If you cannot confirm yet, start with a mixed test container: infrared for volume, induction for premium — small MOQ, real sell-through data before you scale.
