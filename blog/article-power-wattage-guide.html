Why Your Customers' Induction Cookers Keep Dying — and the 3 Specs That Decide If a Unit Survives

📅 Aug 21, 2026⏱ 8 min read✍️ Vortix Kitchen

The complaint is always the same: "I bought a batch, three months in the kitchen and half of them stopped heating." In markets like Central Asia, Southeast Asia and Africa, cookers don't fail because they're badly made in general — they fail because the environment is brutal. Ambient temperatures hit 40°C, dust gets everywhere, and the grid swings. A unit that survives a European living room dies in a Lagos or Almaty commercial kitchen in a season. The importer pays for it in returns, refunds and a ruined reputation.

The good news: every one of these failures is predictable, and every one of them is designed out at the specification stage. Here are the three things that actually kill cookers in your markets — and the exact specs to demand so they don't.

Killer #1: the IGBT overheats and shuts down

The IGBT (the power switch at the heart of every induction cooker) is the component most likely to fail. In a hot kitchen running all day, heat builds faster than it dissipates. Cheap units use a bare-minimum IGBT with a small heatsink and a single thermal cutoff — so they throttle, then shut off mid-service, then die.

What to demand: an oversized IGBT with margin above the rated wattage, a proper aluminum heatsink, and a thermal management system that sheds heat continuously — not just a fuse that trips. For sustained commercial use, look for a unit rated for continuous duty, not just "peak" power.

Killer #2: dust chokes the cooling fan

Induction cookers cool by forced air. In dusty environments the fan intake clogs, airflow drops, and the internals cook themselves. This is the silent killer — the unit looks fine until it isn't.

What to demand: a dust-resistant fan design and, more importantly, a fan that is serviceable. If the only way to replace a $1 fan is to scrap the whole unit, your customers will blame you. Ask whether the fan is a standard, field-replaceable part — and whether spare fans are available as a line item, not a favor.

Killer #3: voltage spikes fry the board

Grids across these regions are unstable. A surge or brownout can take out the control board in a single event. A unit without protection is a lottery ticket.

What to demand: surge protection on the input, plus a wide-voltage tolerance design (a unit that holds steady through brownouts rather than rebooting). For markets with frequent fluctuation, this single spec is the difference between a unit that lasts and a unit that becomes a warranty claim.

The buyer's "survival spec" checklist

  1. IGBT margin: confirm the power device is rated above the unit's continuous wattage, with a real heatsink — not just a thermal fuse.
  2. Serviceable fan: the cooling fan must be a standard, replaceable part, and spares must be orderable.
  3. Surge + wide-voltage: input surge protection and tolerance for grid fluctuation, documented — not claimed.
  4. Continuous-duty rating: for any commercial or all-day use, the unit must be rated for sustained operation, not peak-only.
  5. Ask for the failure data: a serious factory can tell you its field return rate and the top failure modes. If they can't, that's your answer.

Household vs commercial: don't mix them up

A 2000–3000W household unit on 220–240V / 50Hz is the high-volume default and is fine for light daily use. But the moment the use case is a restaurant, a hotel breakfast station or all-day commercial cooking, the survival specs above stop being "nice to have" and become the whole product. Specifying a household unit into a commercial load is the fastest way to a truckload of returns.

The unit that survives your market is not the cheapest one on the quote. It is the one whose IGBT, fan and surge protection were specified for heat, dust and unstable voltage — before it left the factory.
